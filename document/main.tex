\section{Introduction}

\subsection{Autonomous Vehicle Safety Testing}

The commercial deployment of autonomous driving (AD) technologies has been a delayed advancement in the field of autonomous vehicles due to the overall public perception of the uncertainty of their technologies. To combat the societal pushback and issues stemming from a lack of public trust in such innovations, it is necessary to aptly test, prove, quantify and communicate vehicle safety, algorithm robustness, and driver-passenger comfort in autonomous driving tasks.

In the typical pipeline of AD testing on the road to deployment, there exist three main testing stages: (1) virtual-based testing, (2) closed-field testing, and (3) on-road testing, with virtual-based testing being relied heavily upon prior to real-world testing, as real-world testing presents challenges to cost, general resources, and long training cycles.

A longstanding area of research, virtual-based testing has been explored across a wide range of software and functionalities, ranging from using simulations of the real-world data collected in the past to automatically making hypothetical test cases in online simulators. One of the gaps in this field is largely present in ensuring the coverage of the testable space in scenario generation methods.

Novel methods of using machine learning and large language models have been at the forefront of innovations within the field of virtual-based testing. Large language models are being used to train a policy to output human-like maneuvers and have successfully shown to do so []. Machine learning methods are also being used to cluster and extrapolate past traffic data (inclusive of high-risk and near-crash situations) to make hypothetical situations for testing in-sim.

The lane-changing and lane-merging autonomous driving task is one of the most notoriously known to be complex to model and ensure robustness due to the considerably larger dimensional feature space governing its execution, especially in comparison to simpler one-dimensional AD tasks such as cruise control. As such, extensive research is currently being carried out in strengthening their robustness on the road. As of current, static lane-changing testing has been officially approved by global standards, such as the ISO; however, dynamic testing remains a field unanswered and under study.

\subsection{Reinforcement Learning}

Reinforcement learning (RL) is a machine learning method that trains its neural networks and policies on maximizing a provided reward function. The reward function is designed with the goal of quantifying desirable and undesirable actions within a target environment. This field of reinforcement learning is one currently being explored for its breadth of application. It is extensively used in autonomous driving research, both as a standalone method from perception to planning to control and as a tool used in conjunction with classical control, with a known combination being the use of reinforcement learning as a high-level controller and classical control as a low-level controller.

\subsubsection{Multi-Agent Reinforcement Learning}

RL, having a repertoire spanning decades of research, has been extended not just to the control of one agent but to multiple agents inhabiting the same environment. Research on multi-agent RL is extensive, being a subgroup of reinforcement learning that in itself also has a wide range of applications and execution methods.

Multi-agent RL may be used in cases with agents of differing homogeneity, objectives, and training methods. Specifically in training vehicle platoons for cooperative maneuver, vehicles are typically treated as a homogeneous entity across the platoon, mainly for simplicity and speed of neural network integration. Whereas for vehicles undergoing non-cooperative zero-sum games, they are usually each treated with a different set of reward functions and separate actor and critic networks.

Due to this, one drawback in the implementation of multi-agent RL is its proneness to the issue of non-stationarity, i.e., having an environment that is non-Markovian due to all agents acting individually based on a changing policy.

In the case of cooperative vehicle platoon, they undergo the philosophy of centralized training and decentralized execution, indicating that while each agent in the environment accounts solely for its own actions, their brain and understanding of the environment is shared with the others; they are aware of the perception and actions of the other agents in addition to theirs, thereby allowing them to make actions based platoon and hivemind-like targets and not just its own.

\subsubsection{Soft Actor-Critic Reinforcement Learning}

Most RL frameworks are equipped with two main network types: (1) the actor, and (2) the critic. 

The actor is in charge of receiving the observation states from the environment and outputting the best next actions for the agent to take. On the other hand, the critic is in charge of receiving the observation states and the next action, evaluating their pairing, and outputting a Q critic score that quantifies the value of the environment at the instant of the provided observation states and next actions.

Soft actor-critic aims to maximize the entropy of exploration during training of the policy prior to convergence by introducing three unique features:

Introducing a temperature parameter that artificially spikes the entropy when exploration scope gets too small
By using probabilistic decision-making in the actor networks, outputting mean and standard deviation that represent a normal distribution
Utilizing a memory buffer that accounts for previously saved data

The training of both the actor and critic networks follows the pipeline displayed in Figure __. During each training step, a large batch of samples (including the observation, action, reward, and next observation) is procured, and the pipeline is run for each sample.

First, the next action is taken by running the next observation through the current actor network. Then, the critic network evaluates this next observation-next action pairing to find a Q score, which is then consolidated with the obtained reward using the Bellman equation, as seen in Equation __. From here, the true target value is obtained.


The critic network is also used to obtain a Q score for the observation-action pairing, and the resulting value is combined with the true target value to get a TD error, which may be calculated as seen in Equation __.


From this, the minimum squared-error (MSE) loss function is calculated, and the obtained error is plugged into the gradient ascent equation and backpropagation for the critic network.

The current observation and action are then run through the newly updated critic network to find a Q score, which is utilized in calculating loss and gradient ascent for the actor network, as seen in Equation __.

The cycle repeats for however many samples were taken for that training step.

\section{Proposed Methodology}

This study aims to utilize a multi-agent soft-actor critic reinforcement learning framework in training an adversarial platoon to bully a target vehicle on a highway, as a proof of concept for RL-induced environments as a data generation tool, which, in this case, is for edge cases in AD testing.

The objective of the aforementioned method is to evaluate whether a platoon of multiple vehicles designed to create near-crash scenarios with the target vehicle (hereafter referred to as the ego) will be an effective method in generating test cases that quantify the limits of new autonomous driving technologies.

\section{Definition of the Problem}

In a highway environment consisting of a merging lane, a cruising lane, and an exit lane, as shown in the schematic in Figure {}, an ego vehicle equipped with a baseline Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) and Minimizing Overall Braking Induced by Lane changes (MOBIL) lane-changing (LC) model is tasked to sequentially merge into the cruising lane, cruise, and then finally exit onto the exit lane.

At the same time, two adversarial vehicles are instantiated and placed at arbitrary starting longitudinal positions at the beginning of the cruising lane. These two adversaries are equipped with MASAC-RL and are tasked to induce near-crash situations in the lane-changing model as it is traversing the highway, all while ensuring that the lane-changer is able to successfully exit the highway cruising lane.

\section{Premature Non-Convergence}

The action policy of MASAC-RL starts with randomized decision-making and progressively converges to a policy that maximizes the reward received from the environment. However, due to its entropy-maximizing feature, rather than relying on the converged policy to be able to output scenarios that present high risk upon its deployment, the training of the policy was made to be the source of the test-case farming. Average rewards per episode were seen to spike prior to convergence due to the discovery of local maxima before converging to a lower average reward value.

\subsection{Kinematic Bicycle Model}

All vehicles within the Highway-Env library utilized the kinematic bicycle model (KBM) \cite{polack2017kinematic}, governed by the state-space representation in Equation \ref{eq:kbm}.

\begin{equation}
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_{t+1} \\
        y_{t+1} \\
        \theta_{t+1} \\
        v_{t+1} \\
        \gamma_{t+1} \\
    \end{bmatrix} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        x_t + \Delta tv_t\cos{\theta_{t}} \\
        y_t + \Delta tv_t\sin{\theta_{t}} \\
        \theta_t + \Delta t\frac{v_t}{L_b}\tan(\gamma_t) \\
        v_t + \Delta ta_t\ \\
        \gamma_t + \Delta t \omega_t
    \end{bmatrix}
    \label{eq:kbm}
\end{equation}


The vehicles are all controlled using two controllers, one handling longitudinal control and one handling lateral control. The longitudinal controller is a baseline proportional controller \cite{highway-env}, as seen in Equation \ref{eq:longitudinal_controller}. 

\begin{equation}
    a = K_p(v_{\text{ref}} - v)
    \label{eq:longitudinal_controller}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{list}{-}{spacing}
    \item $a$ is the acceleration (throttle),
    \item $v$ is the current velocity,
    \item $K_p$ is the proportional gain,
    \item $v_{\text{ref}}$ is the reference velocity.
\end{list}

For the lateral controller, a proportional-derivative controller is used \cite{highway-env}, as seen in Equations \ref{eq:lateral_position_controller} and \ref{eq:lateral_heading_controller}.

\begin{equation}
    v_{\mathrm{lat, r}} = -K_{\mathrm{p, lat}}\Delta_{\mathrm{lat}}
    \label{eq:lateral_position_controller}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
    \dot{\psi}_r = K_{p, \psi}(\psi_r - \psi)
    \label{eq:lateral_heading_controller}
\end{equation}

where

\begin{list}{-}{spacing}
    \item $K_{\mathrm{p, lat}}$ is the proportional gain for lateral position,
    \item $K_{p, \psi}$ is the proportional gain for heading angle,
    \item $v_{\mathrm{lat, r}}$ is the lateral velocity command,
    \item $\Delta_{\mathrm{lat}}$ is the lateral distance from the lane centerline,
    \item $\psi_r = \psi_L + \Delta\psi_r$ is the reference heading angle,
    \item $\psi_L$ is the lane heading (at some look-ahead position to anticipate turns),
    \item $\Delta\psi_r = \arcsin\frac{v_{\mathrm{lat, r}}}{v}$ is the heading angle error,
    \item $\psi$ is the current heading angle.
\end{list}

The final steering angle is calculated to be shown in Equation \ref{eq:steering_angle}.

\begin{equation}
    \delta = \arcsin\frac{l_r\dot{\psi}_r}{2v},
    \label{eq:steering_angle}
\end{equation} 

where $l_r$ is the distance from the rear axle to the center of mass of the vehicle.

\subsection{Calculation of 2D TTC}

The calculation of the two-dimensional time-to-collision (TTC) values between two vehicles were modeled from the work of Yiru Jiao \cite{jiao2023ttc}, characterized by its computational lightness in comparison to other algorithms with more extrapolative techniques and trajectory-dependent methods calculating for TTC.

The relative distance and relative velocity of both vehicles are taken and calculated for intersection. If the dot product of both vectors is 0, the TTC is calculated. If no intersection is found, the algorithm returns infinity.

\begin{equation}
    \mathrm{TTC} = \frac{\mathrm{DTC}}{\sqrt{v_{\text{rel}}^2 + \epsilon}}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    \mathrm{DTC} & \text{ is the minimum distance between the bounding boxes of vehicles along direction of relative velocity} \\
    v_{\text{rel}} & \text{ is the relative velocity between the two vehicles} \\
    \epsilon & \text{ is a small constant to avoid division by zero}
\end{align}

\section{Contributions: The Platoon Rulebook}

In the formulation of the RL reward function, the proponents developed a list of ground rules by which the platoon vehicles must adhere. This set of rules, hereafter referred to as “The Platoon Rulebook,” is composed of five overarching clauses.

\begin{enumerate}
    \item The adversary must NOT crash itself.
    \item The adversary must KEEP OFF lane and highway BOUNDARIES.
    \item NO REVERSING on the highway.
    \item The adversary must BE MINDFUL of other adversaries’ safety.
    \item The adversary must BULLY the ego WITHOUT crashing.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item The adversary must be as CLOSE TO THE EGO as much as possible.
        \item The adversary must MINIMIZE TTC and THW.
        \item The adversary must try to MATCH THE EGO SPEED.
        \item The adversaries must work together to perform a PLATOONING MANEUVER.
        \item The adversaries must yield to LET THE EGO EXIT.
    \end{enumerate}
\end{enumerate}

The specific equations utilized to model the reward patterns for the above rules, as well as the parameters  and coefficients for the aforementioned reward functions, were chosen at the discretion of the researcher and may be modified as future extensions of this work.

\subsection{Rule 1: No Crashing!}

The Rule 1 reward function, as seen in Equation ??, was modeled after an exponential decay function that gives an adversary with a huge penalty upon crash and continues to provide a penalty decaying in value as the number of steps since the crash increases, signaling to the adversary its missed opportunity in continuing to put the ego at risk and the disadvantage of maintaining a 0 state for its platoon activity reading.

\subsection{Rule 2: Keep Lane!}

The Rule 2 reward function, displayed in Equation ??, was modeled after a cosine function that peaks at no penalty when the vehicle is in the middle of a lane and increases in penalty as it nears lane boundaries.

\subsection{Rule 3: No Reversing!}

The Rule 3 reward function, as seen in Equation ??, is a simple one-time penalty function that activates once the longitudinal velocity of the adversary is detected to be negative.

\subsection{Rule 4: Be Mindful!}

The Rule 4 reward function, displayed in Equation ??, is a function based on adversary-to-adversary TTC, modeled with a difference-of-sigmoids shape that provides the adversaries engaging in low TTC (less than around 4 seconds) with another adversary a penalty, with the reward peaking at a positive reward at around 5 seconds to indirectly increase adversary proximity, and finally falling asymptotically to 0 as TTC goes to infinity.

\subsection{Rule 5a: Ego Proximity!}

The Rule 5a reward function, as seen in Equation ??, is modeled after a double-sigmoid function that rewards adversaries according to their relative distance to the ego, whether they are within risk-conducive range (positive reward), safe range (slightly positive reward), non-risk-conducive range (negative reward), the bounds of which were tuned by the proponent.

\subsection{Rule 5b: Maximize Risk!}

The Rule 5b reward functions are modeled after widely regarded risk metrics TTC, as previously discussed, and time headway (THW), which is defined as an ego vehicle’s time to collision to a target without accounting for the target’s speed. This 2D definition of THW was extended in the same way as that of the TTC, previously discussed.

\subsubsection{Ego TTC Reward Function}

The ego-TTC reward function is similarly modeled by a difference-of-sigmoids shape that provides the adversaries engaging in low TTC with the ego vehicle corresponding rewards. A TTC of less than 1 second incurs a negative reward due to the imminence of a non-recoverable crash, whereas a TTC of within 1 to 4 seconds is incentivized with a positive reward with the peak at around 2 seconds. The reward then goes to 0 as TTC goes to infinity.

\subsubsection{Ego THW Reward Function}

The THW reward function was modeled using an exponential decay function that provides the highest possible reward when time headway is near zero.

These two reward functions aim to teach the network the importance of balance between long-term risk (TTC) and immediate risk (THW).

\subsection{Rule 5c: Speed Matching}

The Rule 5c reward function, as can be seen in Equation ??, is modeled after an exponential decay function with the reward starting at its highest and positive at a relative speed of zero, but progressively decreasing until a negative reward the higher the relative speed becomes.

\subsection{Rule 5d: Platooning}

The Rule 5d reward functions for platooning are split into three parts: (1) proximity reward, (2) swarming reward, and (3) sandwiching reward.

For proximity reward, an ellipse bounding the ego vehicle was established according to the ego’s current speed, and every adversary that enters this aforementioned ellipse receives a reward, with the nearer resulting in a higher reward.

For the swarming reward, the adversaries currently within the ellipse are provided an additional reward for every adversary that enters, the reward itself modeled by an exponential function.

Lastly, for the sandwiching reward, adversaries that are involved in the sandwiching of the ego vehicle (front-back placement) are provided with an additional reward.

\subsection{Rule 5e: Yielding}

The Rule 5e reward function of yielding is composed of a simple one-time penalty function, penalizing all adversaries for an ego crash and rewarding for the ego reaching the exit. The ego may be assumed to be safe from risk in the absence of adversaries due to its utilization of proven baseline IDM and MOBIL models.

\section{Results & Discussions}

The MASAC-RL was trained in the created highway environment, and the exact high-risk moments that led to adversary-ego TTC of less than four seconds were isolated for maneuver classification. For each high-risk moment, trajectory plots spanning four seconds leading up to the high-risk moment were also procured and saved.

The following features were lifted from the observations:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item Relative longitudinal distance with ego
    \item Relative longitudinal speed with ego
    \item Inter-adversary distance
    \item Boolean for currently being in a sandwiching maneuver
    \item Boolean for currently having one adversary crashed or not
\end{enumerate}

The high-risk moments were then run through a K-means clustering algorithm with a total of 11 clusters for grouping according to broad adversarial behaviours. The 11 clusters are seen in Figure ??.

Discarding clusters with relatively fewer data points upon presumption of outlier activity, Clusters _ and _, four broad classifications of high-risk maneuvers were found to have emerged from the clustering, with each containing clusters that hold similar adversary positioning.

These behaviors were identified to be the following:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Cruise blocking, which is risk coming from one adversary in front
    \item Merge blocking, which is risk coming from the creation of a wall in the next lane preventing a safe merge in the ego lane-changer
    \item Intentional fratricide, which is risk coming from the crashing of the adversaries in front
    \item Sandwiching, which is risk coming from the combined front positioning and back positioning of two vehicles
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{Intelligent Driver Model (IDM) + Minimizing Overall Braking Induced by Lane Changes (MOBIL)}

The IDM is a car-following model that describes the longitudinal dynamics of a vehicle based on its desired speed, and the 
distance to the vehicle in front of it. The IDM is defined by the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    a = a_{\text{max}}\left(1 - \left(\frac{v}{v_0}\right)^{\delta} - \left(\frac{s^*(v, \Delta v)}{s}\right)^2\right)
\end{equation}

where
\begin{align}
    a & \text{ is the acceleration of the vehicle} \\
    a_{\text{max}} & \text{ is the maximum acceleration} \\
    v & \text{ is the current speed of the vehicle} \\
    v_0 & \text{ is the desired speed of the vehicle} \\
    \delta & \text{ is the acceleration exponent} \\
    s & \text{ is the gap to the leading vehicle} \\
    s^*(v, \Delta v) & \text{ is the desired minimum gap, a function of the current speed and speed difference} \\
    \Delta v & \text{ is the speed difference to the leading vehicle}
\end{align}

The MOBIL model is a lane-changing model that describes the decision-making process of a vehicle when considering a lane change.
It particularly looks at the acceleration gains and losses of the ego vehicle and its neighboring vehicles in order to evaluate
the safety and incentives of a lane change. The model operates on the rules of (1) the required deceleration of the new follower
not exceeding a safe braking threshold, and (2) the acceleration increase being greater than the total deceleration forced on the new follower.

The latter rule is scaled by a politeness factor \(p\) that represents the ego vehicle's willingness to sacrifice its own acceleration gain 
in order to reduce the deceleration of the new follower.

The MOBIL model is defined by the following equation:

\begin{equation}
    a_{\text{new}} \geq -b_{\text{safe}}
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}
\Delta a_{\text{ego}} + p \left( \Delta a_{\text{new}} + \Delta a_{\text{old}} \right) > a_{\text{th}}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{align}
    \Delta a_{\text{ego}} & \text{ is the acceleration gain of the ego vehicle} \\
    \Delta a_{\text{new}} & \text{ is the acceleration loss of the new follower} \\
    \Delta a_{\text{old}} & \text{ is the acceleration loss of the old follower} \\
    p & \text{ is the politeness factor} \\
    a_{\text{th}} & \text{ is the acceleration threshold}
\end{align}

The field of reinforcement learning (RL) is a subfield of the broader field of machine learning focusing on training an agent
to make the appropriate decisions in a particular environment. Based on the characteristics of the environment, a reward function
is defined and is made to provide the agent with feedback on the appropriateness of its actions. The agent then uses this feedback to 
learn the appropriate policy to take in order to maximize the expected return.

Reinforcement learning has multiple ways of being implemented, with actors and critics being the backbone of its most popular implementations.
The concept of learning by mistake is governed by learning how an environment works \(critic role\) and how to best navigate it (actor role).

room for ambiguity in intuitively choosing appropriate actions when the goal is clearly defined. As such, there is no need for learning the intricacies of
an environment. Rather, the critic simply gives a score to what is currently observed in the environment in order to determine the best course of action.

Actor-only RL implementations, on the other hand, trains an agent how to act based on raw  in environments where either the specific configuration of an action to take in the environment is complex or the environment is partially observable.

Figure \ref{fig:gym_training_pipeline} displays the generalized training process that the Gymnasium library utilizes.

Upon initialization of the environment, one is provided with a pair of outputs, which consists of (1) the initial observation matrix, that quantifies the current state of the environment and its agents, 
and (2) info, which is a dictionary of additional information about the environment, used for debugging and analysis purposes.

With every step of the environment, the current observation matrix is fed into an arbitrary policy to receive a set of actions for which the agents of the environment are then tasked to enact.
Using the actions from the arbitrary policy, they are then fed back into the environment to step the current state into the next state, which then provides a set of five outputs:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item an observation matrix, that quantifies the next state of the environment and its agents;
    \item a reward matrix, that represents the quantifiable results of an agent's actions;
    \item a termination boolean, that indicates whether the episode has just been terminated
    \item a truncation boolean, that indicates whether the episode has just been truncated
    \item info, which is a dictionary of additional information about the updated environment
\end{enumerate}

Typically, in the case of reinforcement learning, the aforementioned arbitrary policy is replaced with a policy that is undergoing training based on the information of 
the current observation matrix, next observation matrix, and the reward matrix that represents whether the actions taken by the agents were appropriate or not. 
The training process is repeated until the policy converges to a robust policy for use in testing and evaluation.

The pipeline of the lane-change maneuver of the ego vehicle involves the separation of the "if," "when," and "how" frameworks of the maneuver, as developed in \cite{7637091}. The "if" pertains to determining whether or not a lane-change maneuver into the next lane is appropriate. The "when" part aims to determine which gap to intrude into and when to begin the move. Lastly, the "how" pertains to the usage of low-level controllers to execute the maneuver according to the determined appropriate gap.

\subsubsection{LC Global Planner}
In the LC, the global planner takes care of the "if" part by utilizing lane-utility values that correspond to appropriateness of a move into the said lane. The four utility values in this research are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item Average travel time in the lane
    \item Average time gap density in the lane
    \item Remaining travel time in the lane
    \item Urgency of reaching the lane in achieving end position
\end{enumerate}

The four equations representing the above normalized lane-utility values $U_{\mathrm{avetravtime}}$, $U_{\mathrm{avetimegap}}$, $U_{\mathrm{remtravtime}}$, $U_{\mathrm{urgency}}$ are detailed as follows:
\begin{equation}
    d_{\mathrm{max}} = \beta v_{\mathrm{ref}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{avetravtime}} = \frac{-\left| \frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{\max(\gamma,v_{\mu})} \right|}{\left| \frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}} - \frac{d_{\mathrm{max}}}{\gamma} \right|}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{avetimegap}} = \frac{\min(\alpha t_{\mathrm{gap, desired}}, t_{\mathrm{gap, \mu}})}{\alpha t_{\mathrm{gap, desired}}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{remtravtime}} = \frac{\frac{\min(d_{\mathrm{max}}, d_{\mathrm{end}})}{v_{\mathrm{ref}}}}{\frac{d_\mathrm{max}}{v_\mathrm{ref}}}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    U_{\mathrm{urgency}} = \exp \left( -\kappa\frac{i_{\mathrm{current}}+1}{n_{\mathrm{lanes}}+1} \cdot \Delta s \right) 
\end{equation}

The four utility values are then consolidated as seen in Equation \eqref{eq:totalutility}.

\begin{equation} \label{eq:totalutility}
    U_{\mathrm{total}} = \sum\limits_{i \in A} w_{i}U_{i}
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
    A = \{\mathrm{avetravtime, avetimegap, remtravtime, urgency}\}
\end{equation}\

The lane with the highest utility values is then chosen as the target/desired lane, as seen in Equation \eqref{eq: desiredlane}.

\begin{equation} \label{eq: desiredlane}
    L_{\mathrm{desired}} = \argmax\limits_{l \in L} \left(U_{l}-(1+\zeta|l|)\left|U_0\right|\right)
\end{equation}

The global planner is then made to choose the best inter-vehicle traffic gap in the desired lane and the time instance in which it shall enact the lane intrusion. To achieve such, a "time-position"-area, is formulated, corresponding to the intersection between the reachable set of the ego car given its dynamical limitations (current velocity, allowed acceleration values, etc.), and the predicted safe lane change region, which is defined as the space that isn't occupied by any vehicle currently and across the prediction horizon.

The "time-position"-areas across the discretized horizon are summed and accumulate into a final "gap utility" value, displayed as $A_g$. The gap with the largest gap utility value, as seen in Equation \eqref{eq: gaputility}, is chosen. 

\begin{equation} \label{eq: gaputility}
    \begin{split}
        A_g = \sum\limits^{K}_{k=0}\max\left(0,\min\left(x^{\mathrm{max}}_{E_k},\min\limits_{i \in F_g}(x_{i_k}-[t_{\mathrm{gap,min}}\min(v_{x_{\mathrm{max}}}, v_{x_{i_k}})+d_s])\right) \right. \\
        \left. -\max\left(x^\mathrm{min}_{E_k},\max\limits_{i \in B_g}(x_{i_k}+[t_{\mathrm{gap,min}}v_{x_{i_k}}+d_s])\right)\right)
    \end{split}
\end{equation}

\begin{itemize}
    \item $t_{\mathrm{gap,min}}$ is the minimum allowed time gap to the chosen vehicle
    \item $v_{x_{\mathrm{max}}}$ is the maximum allowed velocity for the ego
    \item $x_{i_k}$ and $v_{x_{i_k}}$ refer to the predicted position and velocity values of the given vehicle, respectively
    \item $d_s$ is a safe-distance buffer
    \item $x^{\mathrm{max}}_{E_k}$ and $x^{\mathrm{min}}_{E_k}$ are the highest and lowest reachable longitudinal distances for the ego, respectively
\end{itemize}

The minimum time (discretized) that it takes for a lane change maneuver must also be accounted for. and must require that the amount of discretized time that the gap utility value exists is greater than or equal to the said minimum discretized time, as seen in Equation \eqref{eq: gaputilityduration}

\begin{equation} \label{eq: gaputilityduration}
    k^{\mathrm{end}}_{A_g}-k^{\mathrm{start}}_{A_g}\geq k_{\mathrm{min}}
\end{equation}

Moreover, the time instance at which the move is initiated is chosen by taking the minimum magnitude of the required control signals (e.g., acceleration/deceleration to reach the "time-position"-area starting from $k_{\mathrm{Peri}}$.

\begin{equation}
    k^{\mathrm{start}}_{A_g} \le k^{\mathrm{Peri}} \le K - k_{\mathrm{min}} 
\end{equation}

The critic network aims to critique the action that an entity takes. This is done by taking a set of data per iteration under review (initial state, action taken, reward received, and final state), taking the current state and action taken, running them through the critic network to receive an evaluatory Q value (i.e., experimental value), which is then compared with the Bellman-equation-derived Q value (i.e., theoretical value), calculated using the final states. The loss (i.e., error) is then calculated and used to determine how the weights in the neural network should be altered (via backpropagation).


\begin{equation}
    Q(s,a)=r+\gamma \max\limits_{a'}Q(s',a')
\end{equation}

Now, this process is done over a sample of iterations, not just a singular iteration under review.

\begin{equation}
    Target = Reward + \gamma Critic(Next State 's, Actor(Next State 's))
\end{equation}


The total reward $R_t$ at time step $t$ is formulated as the sum of four distinct components representing Time-to-Collision (TTC) proximity pressure, goal node achievement, ego vehicle crash penalties, and adversarial vehicle crash penalties:
\begin{equation}
    R_t = R_{\text{TTC}} + R_{\text{goal}} + R_{\text{ego\_crash}} + R_{\text{adv\_crash}}
\end{equation}
